# Scene 21

## The Muse

### Madhavi's House

[Madhavi sits exhausted in her room with little Manimekalai... Vasanthamalai enters.]

Madhavi : Vasanthamalai! What happened? Did you see him?

Vasanthamalai : Did I not tell you then that play would turn into calamity? Did you listen? They say Kovalan and Kannagi have gone toward Madurai... Devandhi told me!

Madhavi : Ah!.... He has gone? Manimekalai... leaving you and me, your loving father has left even this town, my treasure! He would gather you into his arms and shower you with a rain of kisses, saying, “O new moon, white as butter... O fruit of Tamil that lets fall words for me!” They say he came to hate you and me... and has gone away thinking he should not remain even in this Poompuhar where this sinner lives... Your father, who would ache with longing and ask again and again, “When will you call me ‘Appa’?... When will you open your mouth and say ‘Amma’?”—he has gone away saying he does not wish to taste your baby-lisp of refined Tamil, leaving your mother burning in a fierce fire, my child... He has gone!

Chitrapathi : Madhavi, what is this? You wail and weep as though a wedded husband had abandoned you and gone away!....

Madhavi : Enough, Mother... enough!.... The suffering I endure for having come to be born from your womb is enough... Do not torment me any more...

Hunchback : Chitrapathi... Chitrapathi... what is this?

Madhavi : So you too have come, hunchbacked old woman who ruins a household!... In the Ramayana there was one hunchbacked old woman... they say she ruined an entire country; you... have come here to ruin this house itself.

Hunchback : Madhavi... restrain your speech!...

Madhavi : Why?... For what?... Sinners! Was it not all of you together who, without my knowing and without his knowing, plundered all the wealth of his household? Did I love him for wealth?

Chitrapathi : You did not love for wealth... but we let you love him for wealth.

Madhavi : Enough, enough! A demon who came in the form of a mother to swallow me alive; and before that, a hunchbacked fiend who set out to destroy the city of Puhar! I should not even have to open my eyes upon your faces; go away... leave this place and go!

[To the child]

My child Manimekalai, have you seen what has become of us? We have no one at all to support us, my child... no support!

[She sobs and weeps.]

## Translation notes

- `கலைமகள்` is rendered as **`The Muse`**, preserving the title's personification of art rather than turning it into a biographical label such as `Madhavi the Artist`.
- Madhavi's lament deliberately retains Kalaignar's extravagant child-language imagery. `என்னைச் சொல் உதிர்க்கின்ற தமிழ்க் கனியே` becomes **`O fruit of Tamil that lets fall words for me!`**, and `செந்தமிழ்மழலை` becomes **`baby-lisp of refined Tamil`**. Tamil remains part of intimate motherhood, not merely a linguistic label.
- `முத்தமாரி` is retained as a **rain of kisses**, preserving the source image.
- Chitrapathi's `கட்டிய கணவன்` is translated as `wedded husband`; the point is her attempt to deny Madhavi the emotional legitimacy normally granted to a wife. The translation does not endorse that denial.
- Madhavi's comparison of the household hunchback with the Ramayana hunchback is kept exactly as her polemical comparison. No external epic correction or explanation is inserted into the dramatic text.
- Chitrapathi's admission—`நாங்கள் பொருளுக்காகத்தான் உன்னைக் காதலிக்க விட்டோம்`—is kept starkly as **`we let you love him for wealth`**. It confirms exploitation by Madhavi's own household and is not softened into vague family pressure.
- The scene's repeated `போதும்` and final `ஆதரவில்ல` are retained as repetition rather than compressed.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

Scene 21 makes inherited social position and economic exploitation collide with Madhavi's own claim to love. Chitrapathi openly admits that the household allowed Madhavi's relationship because of Kovalan's wealth. Madhavi answers by rejecting both the money logic and the authority of the very family structure that profited from her. That gives the scene a strong dignity and anti-hierarchical resonance: human affection is set against a system that treats a woman's relationship as a source of household income.

Kalaignar also continues to make Tamil an intimate human presence. Manimekalai's first words are imagined as `செந்தமிழ்மழலை`, and the child herself is a `தமிழ்க் கனி`. The movement-era resonance is therefore not only public language pride; Tamil is located inside motherhood, tenderness and personal loss.

The Ramayana reference is used by Madhavi as a weapon in a family quarrel. The note does not turn that single comparison into a broader anti-epic claim that the scene itself does not make.
