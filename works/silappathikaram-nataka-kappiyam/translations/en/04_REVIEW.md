## 2. Kalaignar-language review

PASS.

The translation preserves the scene's emotional and rhetorical escalation: renunciation is first a family shock, then a moral sacrifice, then an explicit argument against fate, and finally a satire on the defeated soothsayer.

Protected rhetorical images include:

- `தாய்மையுள்ளங் கொண்ட அண்ணன்` → `an elder brother with a mother's heart`;
- `ஈட்டிகள் ஆயிரத்தை எங்கள் இதயத்தில் பாய்ச்சாதே!` → `Do not drive a thousand spears into our hearts!`;
- `பெற்ற வயிறு புலம்புவதைக்` → `hear the womb that bore you lament`;
- `வேதனைக் களமாகி விடும் தமிழ்நாடு!` → `the Tamil land will become a field of suffering!`;
- `இமயத்தின் நெற்றியிலே` → `upon the brow of the Himalaya`;
- repeated `விதியை எதிர்க்கிறேன்` → repeated `I oppose fate`.

The translation does not soften the family's grief or Ilango's confrontational certainty.

## 3. Fate / reason terminology review

PASS.

The central source opposition is preserved as:

- `விதி` → `fate`;
- `மதி` → `reason`.

The key line remains deliberately compact:

`This is not fate, Mother! Fate is that I should become king! This—this is reason!`

The later `விதியின் வீழ்ச்சி! மதியின் வெற்றி!` becomes `The fall of ... fate! The victory of reason!`, retaining parallel structure and ideological dramatic force.

`ஊழ்வினை` in the soothsayer exchange is handled contextually as fate rather than expanded into a doctrinal explanation of karma absent from the scene.

## 4. Tamil geography / historical rhetoric review

The source uses both `தமிழ்நாடு` and `தமிழகம்`. The translation correctly distinguishes them:

- `தமிழ்நாடு` → `the Tamil land` in this ancient dramatic context;
- `இன்பத் தமிழகம்` → `this joyous Tamilakam`.

The Chera bow-banner on the Himalaya remains a source-supported historical-rhetorical image and is not reduced to generic military success.

PASS.

## 5. Dravidian-context review

PASS WITH SOURCE/INTERPRETATION SEPARATION.

The relation is unusually direct because the source itself explicitly opposes fate and reason, argues against passive submission to destiny, and satirizes the soothsayer after his prediction fails.

The interpretive note therefore relates the scene to Dravidian rationalism, human agency and critique of fatalist authority. It does not insert movement labels into dialogue and does not erase the source's simultaneous presence of family affection, renunciation and divine/fate language spoken by other characters.

## 6. Tone / humour review

The final exchange preserves Kalaignar's comic deflation. After the scene's high family grief and ideological declaration, the soothsayer worries that his livelihood and honour are gone. The English retains that abrupt tonal turn rather than making him solemn.

PASS.
