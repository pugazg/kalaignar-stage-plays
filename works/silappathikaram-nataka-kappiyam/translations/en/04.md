# Scene 4

## Ilango's Renunciation

### Cheralathan's Palace

[Cheralathan, Narchonai, Senguttuvan and Venmal are there. Senguttuvan's face is now bright and clear.]

Senguttuvan : Who is Ilango? My younger brother, is he not? Think of me as though I had never been born and crown him!...

Cheralathan : While the elder lives, to give it to the younger... that is not the proper order, Senguttuva!

Senguttuvan : Has not the soothsayer said that such is divine will! Father! Let the land belong to my younger brother! I will not stand in the way of what is to come to him by the path of fate! I shall remain commander of the army and protect both the land and my brother.....

[A soft voice calls, “Elder brother,” and everyone turns.

Ilango stands transformed into Ilango Adigal!

The garb of renunciation!....]

Senguttuvan : Brother! What is this garb?

Ilango : The garb of renunciation that a younger brother has taken for an elder brother with a mother's heart! Ilango is no king; Ilango is Adigal!....

Narchonai : Ilango, my cherished son, was it fate that I should have to see you like this?

Ilango : This is not fate, Mother! Fate is that I should become king! This—this is reason!

Senguttuvan : Brother! Do not wear this garb! Accept the Chera empire! You who fill my whole heart! You, who should ride in procession through the streets of beautiful Vanchi like a proud bull—do not don the garb of renunciation and leave us writhing!...

Cheralathan : Beloved son! You have opened and shown us your heart of sacrifice! You have made your fame waft like fragrance across all the southern land! Think—after raising you to rule, is it right to drive us mad?...

Narchonai : Yes, my dear! Ilango! Do not drive a thousand spears into our hearts!

[Venmal leans against a pillar, shedding tears.]

Look there—your sister-in-law weeping....... your father trembling....... your brother shaken....... hear the womb that bore you lament, my son, hear it!

Ilango : Let no one stop me!... This is the decision I have chosen in order to conquer fate! If we say, “Let us walk only along the path fate has left us,” the Tamil land will become a field of suffering! If Father had thought victory and defeat were only as fate decreed.... he could never have flown the bow-banner upon the brow of the Himalaya! I oppose fate.... I oppose it more and more! This is the victory I have won at the very outset! Bless me and send me on my way!...

Senguttuvan : Ilango! Where are you going?

Ilango : I will not go anywhere beyond this joyous Tamilakam. I am going to stay at Kunavayir Kottam, near Tilakanagar! I will meet all of you often.

Father!...

Mother!...

Sister-in-law!...

Elder brother!...

I take my leave......

[Tears well and overflow in everyone's eyes]

[Ilango Adigal leaves, renouncing the palace.]

Outside the palace entrance.

[All the guards watch Ilango Adigal depart with tears in their eyes! Men of letters and poets alike stand like trees in a sorrowful silence. Women, children, farmers and merchants become as still as painted figures. From the palace, mother, father, elder brother and sister-in-law watch. Ilango Adigal has gone far into the distance........ The soothsayer comes toward him.]

Soothsayer : Who? Ilango?... What is this?...

Ilango : The fall of that fate of which you spoke so proudly! The victory of reason! Hang your defeated face, abandon even your profession, and come—follow me.

Soothsayer : Ah!...

Ilango : Why, can you not?.... Has your heart not ripened? Is fate itself holding you back? I take my leave!

[The soothsayer, astonished, speaks to himself]

Soothsayer : Now even my livelihood is gone; if I go near the palace again, my honour will be gone too. Defeat! A great defeat!!

[The scene ends with the soothsayer standing bewildered.]

## Translation notes

- `இளங்கோ துறவு` is rendered as `Ilango's Renunciation`; the English keeps the action and choice central rather than treating renunciation as merely a costume change.
- `இளங்கோ அடிகள்` is retained as `Ilango Adigal`. `அடிகள்` functions here as a reverential ascetic title and as part of the historical-literary name by which Ilango is known; flattening it to `monk` would erase that function.
- `தாய்மையுள்ளங் கொண்ட அண்ணன்` becomes `an elder brother with a mother's heart`, preserving Kalaignar's striking fusion of fraternal and maternal affection.
- The pivotal antithesis `விதி ... மதி` is translated as `fate ... reason`: `This is not fate, Mother! Fate is that I should become king! This—this is reason!` The compact opposition is intentionally kept blunt and theatrical.
- The source uses both `தமிழ்நாடு` and `தமிழகம்` in this scene. They are not collapsed into one English term: `தமிழ்நாடு` is rendered contextually as `the Tamil land`, while `இன்பத் தமிழகம்` remains `this joyous Tamilakam`.
- `இமயத்தின் நெற்றியிலே விற்கொடியைப் பறக்கவிட்டிருக்க முடியாது` is preserved as `flown the bow-banner upon the brow of the Himalaya`, retaining Kalaignar's bodily-geographic metaphor.
- `நாவலர், பாவலர்` is rendered as `men of letters and poets`, keeping the paired intellectual/literary groups without inventing specific offices.
- `மானம்` in the soothsayer's comic final aside is `honour`, not movement-specific `self-respect`; the immediate context is personal loss of face after public defeat.
- The photograph caption on scan 23 remains source-visual provenance and is not inserted into dramatic translation.
- No published English edition was used in drafting or reviewing this scene.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

Scene 4 contains one of the clearest rationalist-human-agency formulations in Kalaignar's adaptation. Ilango does not merely say that fate can be resisted; he stages the opposition in two compact words: `விதி` and `மதி`—**fate and reason**. He then argues that passive submission to fate would turn the Tamil land into a field of suffering and cites historical human action—his father's Himalayan victory—as evidence that achievement is made through action, not surrendered to predestination.

The closing encounter sharpens the satire: the soothsayer's `fate` has fallen, `reason` has won, and Ilango tells him to abandon the very profession built on prophetic certainty. The soothsayer's final concern is not metaphysical truth but livelihood and public honour. That comic deflation strongly resonates with the Dravidian movement's rationalist critique of fatalism, ritual-authority claims and professions that derive authority from predicting destiny.

A second resonance lies in Kalaignar's repeated Tamil geography: `தமிழ்நாடு`, the Chera historical memory of the Himalaya, and `இன்பத் தமிழகம்`. Human agency is therefore framed not abstractly but within a Tamil historical and civilizational imagination.

The translation preserves those source-supported features but does not insert `atheism`, `Self-Respect Movement`, `Dravidian`, `anti-priestcraft`, or other later ideological labels into the characters' dialogue.
