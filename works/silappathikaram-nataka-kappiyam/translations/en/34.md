# Scene 34

## Uttering the Vow of Vengeance

### Mandapam on the Street

[Kannagi, having heard the news that Kovalan has been cut down, comes running in a tremble. She falls upon the body of her slain husband and cries out in grief.]

Kannagi : Ayyayyo, my dear! My dear! My dear! Have they made you into a corpse, my dear? I sent you to Madurai saying good times would come! Is this that good time, my dear! Why, my dear, were you unjustly killed? Why, my dear, did you admit to a crime you did not commit? Did you make yourself the substance of such a sight thinking, “Let Kannagi cry herself to death, let her float in tears, let her heart burst and scatter like mustard cast into hot oil”? My dear! Did not the noble face that displayed the moon's radiance, the great gaze shown by those blue eyes, and the great beauty shown by your rows of pearl-like teeth proclaim that you were without stain? O honey-nectar, O famed pearl of the South, O my life, O golden lamp! Have you given up your life? Was it for this that you said, “Kannagi, walk with the anklet; I shall delight in seeing you one last time”? Ayyo! Then I did not know that such cruelty lay waiting! Then I did not know, my dear! After perfuming you with akil smoke, spreading sandalwood from the Pothigai Mountain over your gold-glowing body, taking a parrot-green leaf, adding fresh kizhichal lime and areca nut, folding taamboolam and giving it to you, after savouring all the delight of those red lips where laughter blossomed, I sent you on your way! “You will come, you will come,” I stood with my eyes fixed upon the road. You deceived me—

My dear, you deceived me! My dear! What wrong did I do? Why was I born to receive this punishment? Tell me, my dear, tell me! Will you not speak, my dear? You would fondle me saying, “O stainless gold, O right-spiralled pearl, O flawless fragrance, O sugarcane, O honey”—open that kovvai-fruit mouth and speak, my dear! My dear, speak just once, my dear! My dear! My dear! Your Kannagi has come, my dear! Speak, my dear! Will you not speak! Will you not speak? Enough.

[Kannagi rises like an enraged tiger.]

Kannagi : Enough! I shall make this whole world speak for you. You are no thief. O thief who stole my heart, you are no thief. The world must accept this. The Pandya who holds the bent sceptre in his hand must bow his head. I must take vengeance on the unjust men who left you rolling in a mire of blood. Madurai moothaar—the Madurai moothaar that made stainless you a criminal and diminished its own fame—must perish. It must perish root and all. Vengeance for vengeance! Vengeance for vengeance! My dear! Your blood is witness.

[She takes the blood that has flowed over the cut-down Kovalan and marks her forehead with it as a tilak.]

I am coming. I am coming in a moment.

[Kannagi, her eyes spitting fire, removes the other anklet still upon her foot, takes it in her hand and runs toward the Pandya palace like an enraged tiger.]

## Translation notes

- `வஞ்சினங் கூறுதல்` is rendered **`Uttering the Vow of Vengeance`**. The title points to Kannagi's spoken vow after grief turns into fury; the English keeps both the utterance and retaliatory force visible.
- Every major `அத்தான்` beat is preserved through repeated **`my dear`**. The repetition is structural to Kannagi's lament and is not reduced for smoother English.
- `எண்ணெயிட்ட கடுகு போல் இதயம் வெடித்துச் சிதறட்டும்` is kept as the violent domestic image **`let her heart burst and scatter like mustard cast into hot oil`** rather than abstracted to `let her heart break`.
- The farewell memory is retained in full: akil smoke, Pothigai sandalwood, parrot-green leaf, fresh `kizhichal` lime, areca nut, taamboolam and the remembered red lips. `கிழிஞ்சல்` is kept as **`kizhichal`** within `kizhichal lime` because this verified source form is not silently replaced by a guessed preparation term.
- Kovalan's earlier praise string is translated consistently with scene 8: **`stainless gold`, `right-spiralled pearl`, `flawless fragrance`, `sugarcane`, `honey`**.
- The source's column transition deliberately repeats the thought `என்னை ஏமாற்றிவிட்டீர்களே / அத்தான், என்னை ஏமாற்றி விட்டீர்களே!`; the English likewise keeps the repetition across the paragraph break.
- `நீங்கள் கள்வரல்ல. என் உள்ளம் கவர்ந்த கள்வரே, நீங்கள் கள்வரல்ல.` becomes **`You are no thief. O thief who stole my heart, you are no thief.`** The thief/heart-thief reversal is preserved rather than explained away.
- `வளைந்த செங்கோல்` remains **`the bent sceptre`**, continuing the righteous-rule symbolism established in scene 31.
- The verified source prints `மதுரை மூதார்` twice. Because the exact social/toponymic force of this form is not secure enough to justify silently changing the Tamil to another word, the English retains **`Madurai moothaar`** and lets the surrounding sentence carry the immediate target and destructive force.
- `பழிக்குப்பழி! பழிக்குப்பழி!` remains the doubled **`Vengeance for vengeance! Vengeance for vengeance!`**. This is Kannagi's character speech at the instant grief becomes fury; it is not softened into a generalized call for justice.
- The blood-forehead action, removal of the remaining anklet and run toward the palace are kept as explicit stage actions and are not summarized.
- No published English edition was used in drafting or reviewing this scene.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

Scene 34 turns private bereavement into public accusation. Kannagi first reconstructs the ordinary intimacy of her farewell in painful detail; only after exhausting the repeated appeals to `my dear` does she rise and insist that the world itself must acknowledge that Kovalan is no thief. The transition matters: her public agency grows directly out of the refusal to let an imposed criminal label become the truth about him.

The `bent sceptre` links her fury to the justice crisis already established in scene 31. Her target is not only the men who killed Kovalan but the Pandya rule whose act has made the innocent man a criminal. That can resonate with Dravidian-movement concerns with dignity, accountable power and resistance to humiliation, and with the centrality of a woman's voice in confronting authority.

At the same time, `Vengeance for vengeance` and the blood-tilak are Kannagi's own escalating dramatic acts. The translation does not convert them into a later political programme or insert movement vocabulary into her speech.
