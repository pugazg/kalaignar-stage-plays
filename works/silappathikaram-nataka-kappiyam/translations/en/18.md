# Scene 18

## Indra Festival — In Two Households

[Contains several internal scenes]

(The Indra Festival begins in the city of Puhar.

The tiger-banner is raised toward the sky.

The drum upon an elephant...

The whole city celebrates...

A great statue of **Indran, the ancient Tamil king**, rises like a tower touching the sky, as though he were riding upon a tiger.

Another enormous statue rises sky-high, as though Karikalan were standing upon the summit of the Himalaya.

Patti mandapam, pearl pavilion, festooned gateways—celebration everywhere.

Along the shore, a procession of ships....

Women and men, dance and song.

In many places,
in many forms,
celebrations!)

### Madhavi's House

Kovalan adorns Madhavi... Choosing which of the ornaments there is the finest and placing it upon her becomes a great task for him!

Madhavi adorns Kovalan too... The two set out, embodiments of beauty.....

In the hall they lift Manimekalai from the cradle and kiss her...

Manimekalai has grown, smiling...

She laughs, opening her toothless little mouth!]

Kovalan : Look at that laugh—look at it! Madhavi! Is not Manimekalai just like a portrait drawn after looking at you...?

Madhavi : No!... Everyone says Manimekalai looks exactly like elder sister Kannagi...

Kovalan : [Teasingly] Hmm.......

Madhavi : Yes!... Even I think of her as elder sister's own child.

Kovalan : All right ... it is getting late ... come... let us go.

[She kisses the child ... places her back in the cradle... leaves the companions there beside her and departs.]

[Festival scenes in many places—koothu... excitement!... Madhavi and Kovalan join in.]

### Kannagi's House

The sound of the festival tears at Kannagi's ears!

The fireworks bursting in the sky seize her eyes like lightning!...

The dancing of the women ... appears to her like a dance of spirits. The songs of the male and female musicians.... seem like the cries of owls and night-birds...

Kannagi covers her eyes and ears, cries out and falls.

In her memory ... she recalls how, on an Indra Festival day, she and her husband Kovalan once danced and sang in happiness.

The events of the Indra Festival plunge her still deeper into a sea of sorrow: she trembles,

...she wilts... she runs to Kovalan's portrait....]

Kannagi : My dear!... Today you must surely have come to the festival. This life of mine will remain in me only if I see your sacred face at least once!... Here I come... wherever you may be... I shall look upon you, even if only from far away, and return...

My dear!....
My dear!!...
My dear!!!...

[She runs out of the house—Devandhi sees her and runs behind, crying “Kannagi” ... “Kannagi”!...

Kannagi runs calling “My dear”.... “My dear”....
Devandhi follows.]

### Seashore

[Madhavi and Kovalan walk happily, the waves striking at their feet]

[Kannagi comes running. Devandhi follows]

Devandhi : Kannagi!... Stop!...

[At the loud cry, Kannagi stops motionless without turning]

Devandhi : Kannagi!... How did you find such courage? The whole town is at the Indra Festival!... If someone sees you now, have you come seeking disgrace for the family?

Kannagi : Disgrace... Is it disgrace for me to go and see my husband?

Devandhi : At a time like this... in this condition... may a woman from a respected household go out like this? If anyone sees you, what will they think? If your husband Kovalan himself sees you, how angry will he become?

You conduct yourself without showing your pain before your mother and father... If they saw you like this, would they not melt with sorrow for you?...

You were born into a great household... and entered another great household; you are a **Tamil woman who guards honour**—may you lose heart like this?

Come... Kannagi!... Return home!...

Must your husband be before your eyes for him to be yours?... Is he not within your heart!......

[Kannagi sighs deeply and weeps]

Come... Kannagi...

[Devandhi takes her hand and leads her away... Kannagi turns like a lifeless object...]

[The festival continues... The twenty-eight-day Indra Festival comes to an end.... The tiger-banner is lowered...]

### Bedchamber in Madhavi's House

[Madhavi and Kovalan are upon the bed... A lamp is burning. Dawn has not yet broken; the rooster crows... the conch of the hour sounds... Madhavi awakens...]

Madhavi : My dear!...

[She calls... Kovalan does not rise...]

(She takes the yazh and plays it; Kovalan rises...)

Madhavi : My dear ... today makes twenty-eight days... the Indra Festival is ending... Come... let us go to the seashore and savour the sight of dawn!

Kovalan : I am coming ... there you must sing Kaanal Vari and delight me...

Madhavi : You must sing too...

Kovalan : About you, among women... I shall sing. But if I must sing Kaanal Vari about the seashore...

Madhavi : If that is how it is ... then I too will not sing.....

Kovalan : All right ... all right... come! We shall both sing....

Madhavi : Vasanthamalai!

(She comes)

Let the yazh be made ready ... we must reach the shore before dawn... Have the horse-drawn chariot made ready as well!... At the shore ... beneath the shade of the punnai tree ... upon the spread of fresh sand ... arrange a white-legged couch beneath a canopy with a painted screen, so that we may sit and sing Kaanal Vari!...

Vasanthamalai : At once—in a single moment.......

## Translation notes

- The source itself has no single top-level setting, so the English preserves the internal movement through Puhar, Madhavi's house, Kannagi's house, the seashore and finally Madhavi's bedchamber.
- `பழந்தமிழ் மன்னன் இந்திரன்` is translated **`Indran, the ancient Tamil king`**. This follows Kalaignar's own historicizing Tamil framing; no external mythological correction is inserted.
- `புலிக்கொடி` remains the Chola **tiger-banner** rather than being generalized to a festival flag.
- `பட்டி மண்டபம்` is retained as `Patti mandapam`; `முத்துப் பந்தர்` is rendered `pearl pavilion`. The sequence is celebratory architecture, not a modern civic inventory.
- Madhavi's statement that she thinks of Manimekalai as Kannagi's own child is retained directly. It continues the source's sisterhood rather than turning the two households into a simple rivalry.
- Kannagi's sensory distress is translated with the source's violence: festival sound `tears at` her ears, fireworks seize her eyes, and music becomes the cry of night-birds. The festival is not described neutrally from her emotional point of view.
- The triple escalation `அத்தான்! / அத்தான்!! / அத்தான்!!!` remains three separate calls with increasing punctuation.
- `மானங் காக்கும் தமிழச்சி` is rendered **`Tamil woman who guards honour`**. `மானம்` is not automatically converted to the movement-specific `self-respect`; the immediate family/social register remains primary.
- `கானல்வரி` is retained as **`Kaanal Vari`**, a named song/poetic form in this dramatic sequence. Scene 19 will translate the quoted material independently from the verified Tamil source.
- Source-specific forms such as `பார்த்தால்தானு`, `பாடுவதென்றுல்`, and `அப்படியானுல்` are not repaired in Tamil; the English conveys their immediate dramatic sense without pretending the source wording was different.
- No published English edition was used in drafting or reviewing this scene.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

Scene 18 places public Tamil historical spectacle beside two sharply different domestic worlds. The festival displays the tiger-banner, Karikalan and `Indran, the ancient Tamil king`; Kalaignar makes Puhar's civic celebration an act of Tamil historical imagination. That historical recovery has an obvious resonance with later Dravidian cultural politics, but the translation leaves it in the source's own festival imagery rather than adding modern slogans.

The cross-cutting is equally important. Madhavi's house contains beauty, music, Manimekalai and shared festival life; Kannagi's house turns the same public sound and light into pain. Kalaignar does not erase either woman's humanity. Madhavi again calls Manimekalai Kannagi's child, while Devandhi addresses Kannagi as a `Tamil woman who guards honour`. The scene therefore binds Tamil collective identity to women's lived emotional worlds rather than presenting culture as spectacle alone.

The interpretive note does not equate `மானம்` mechanically with Self-Respect Movement terminology, nor does it turn Kannagi's restriction from public space into a modern political speech. The movement-era resonance lies in the Tamil historical framing, the explicit `தமிழச்சி` identity and the continued dignity accorded to both women.
