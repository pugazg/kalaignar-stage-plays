# Scene 5

## The Anklet Takes Form

### Chera Land

[A mountainous, richly wooded region beside the Periyar riverbank. From the millet fields come the sounds of people driving away parrots and the strains of aalolam songs... Signs make clear that this is a region of elephants and tigers.... In the hills, women and men of the mountains walk carrying many kinds of goods upon their heads and shoulders. Baskets, bundles, fawns, tiger cubs, monkeys... they journey with gifts of many kinds.

On the way, at the foot of the hills, they stop before Ilango Adigal and bow to him.]

Ilango Adigal : Tamil people of the hills!.... You seem to be going toward the Periyar riverbank... for what purpose?

A man : Does Adigal not know? Your elder brother, King Senguttuvan, and the poet Sattanar have come to the Periyar riverbank to see the wealth of nature. These are gifts for them...

Ilango : Is that so! I too shall come.

[All continue the journey.]

[At the Periyar riverbank: Senguttuvan, Venmal and Sattanar watch two elephants fighting...

Then—

Ilango and the hill people arrive.]

Senguttuvan : Brother!

Ilango : Elder brother!

[They embrace]

Ilango : Sister-in-law... are you well!

[She nods]

Ilango : Sattanar... you too...

Sattanar : I came to see the wealth of the hills. I have also gained the chance to see you, rich in the wealth of mind.

Ilango : Elder brother! The hill people of this region wish to offer you gifts.

Senguttuvan : Is that so!...

Hill people : Greetings!...

[All bow]

[Before them are heaped elephant tusks, honey, sandalwood logs, coconuts, sugarcane, mangoes, jackfruit, bunches of bananas, a lion cub, a tiger cub, an elephant calf, a kurumpaadu, a fawn, a mongoose, a plumed peacock, a civet cat and a parrot—their gifts.]

Senguttuvan : Many thanks! Joy!! Long live forever the riches of the Tamil land! Dear friends... is there anything remarkable in this region?

A man : There is, there is! A woman who had lost her husband... one named Kannagi... stood for many days under the shade of that vengai tree there... With tears in her eyes she kept saying, “My husband will come, he will come”... Then suddenly, one day, she was nowhere to be seen... Where she went, we do not know. Did her husband—the dead man—return?... Or did she herself go away with him?... We do not know! Never until now have we seen a woman like her....

Senguttuvan : Kannagi...!

Sattanar : Yes! Kannagi, queen of chastity! At the right moment the hill people have reminded us... I shall tell her story. Let Ilango Adigal write it as an epic! Let the Kannagi epic become an incomparable treasury that speaks the glories of the Chera, Chola and Pandya lands...! Since this story belongs to the three crowned kings, it is fitting that Ilango Adigal should write it.

Senguttuvan : Tell it, poet! Ilango will give it literary form! What say you, brother! Is that right?

Ilango : Yes, elder brother!

Sattanar, tell the story of Kannagi...

[Sattanar begins the story while stroking the tiger cub there...]

[Ilango Adigal begins to write.]

[The tiger flag flies. With Sattanar's voice, the scene changes....

Scenes of the city of Puhar begin.]

Sattanar : Puhar—the beautiful great city set where the Kaveri and the sea kiss each other! The peerless jewel of the Chola empire that rules beneath the tiger flag!...

## Translation notes

- `சிலம்பின் தோற்றம்` is rendered as `The Anklet Takes Form`. The source title can point both to the anklet-centred story and to the moment the literary `Silambu` begins to take shape; the English keeps that productive ambiguity rather than reducing it to the physical manufacture of an anklet.
- `ஆலோலப் பாடல்கள்` is retained as `aalolam songs` rather than replaced by a generic `folk songs`; the surrounding stage direction already shows their hill/millet-field setting.
- `மலைவாழ் தமிழர்களே!` is translated directly as `Tamil people of the hills!`, preserving Kalaignar's explicit inclusion of the hill communities within Tamil peoplehood.
- Sattanar's `மலைவளம் / மன வளம்` parallel is retained as `wealth of the hills / wealth of mind`.
- The animal-gift term `குறும்பாடு` is retained as `kurumpaadu` rather than guessed into a modern zoological equivalent. Other clearly identifiable items are translated normally; no source animal is silently omitted.
- `தமிழ்நாட்டு வளங்கள் என்றென்றும் வாழ்க!` becomes `Long live forever the riches of the Tamil land!`, retaining its slogan-like cadence and distinguishing the source's `தமிழ்நாடு` wording from `தமிழகம்` where that term occurs elsewhere.
- `கற்புக்கரசி கண்ணகி` is rendered as `Kannagi, queen of chastity`, preserving the source epithet without adding a modern moral explanation.
- `முடியுடை மூவேந்தர்` becomes `the three crowned kings`, with the immediately named Chera, Chola and Pandya lands supplying the historical frame.
- `இலக்கியம் படைப்பான்` is rendered as `will give it literary form`, preserving the deliberate act of literary creation rather than treating the epic as something that simply appears by destiny.
- `காவிரி யாறும், கடலும் முத்தமிட்டுக் கொள்ளும் இடம்` remains the image of `the Kaveri and the sea kiss each other`; Kalaignar's personified geography is not flattened.
- No published English edition was used in drafting or reviewing this scene.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

Scene 5 shifts from the explicit fate-versus-reason argument of scenes 3–4 into a politics of **Tamil historical belonging and literary memory**. Kalaignar has Ilango address the mountain communities not as an exotic people outside the Tamil world but explicitly as `மலைவாழ் தமிழர்களே`—Tamil people of the hills. Their gifts are then celebrated as the `riches of the Tamil land`.

The story of Kannagi is framed not as the possession of one dynasty but as a treasury that speaks the greatness of all three crowned Tamil polities—Chera, Chola and Pandya. Sattanar tells; Ilango writes; a shared Tamil historical memory is consciously made into literature. That framing strongly resonates with the Dravidian movement's recovery of Tamil antiquity, its civilizational imagination across the three dynasties, and its insistence that Tamil literary history belongs to a broad Tamil people rather than to a narrow court or ritual elite.

The scene also places a woman—Kannagi—at the centre of the literary project. The translation preserves that centrality and the source epithet `queen of chastity`, while the interpretive note does not convert her into a later political symbol beyond what this scene itself supports.

No later ideological terms are inserted into the translated dialogue. The connection to Tamil peoplehood, historical consciousness and literary recovery is stated only here as interpretation.
