# Scene 23

## Madhavi's Renunciation

### Maasathuvan's House

The palm-leaf is in Maasathuvan's hand...

[Kovalan's voice]

“Most beloved! I bow at your feet. By failing in duty to my parents, by causing harm to my own wife, and by becoming the cause of our having to leave for another town to make a living, I myself have brought this about. I ask you to console your heart that grieves over my fault.

Thus,
Yours in love,”

[Maasathuvan sighs deeply...

Then Madhavi comes... with Manimekalai. Four or five women come carrying trays filled with gold coins.]

Maasathuvan : Who is it?

Madhavi : Forgive me... I am Madhavi, the sinner who became the cause of the ruin of your illustrious family! Here! This is the wealth of your household that had been piled up in our house without even my knowing. You must accept it.

Maasathuvan : What is this, Madhavi... Only now have I understood that you are pure gold that loses nothing under assay!

Madhavi : What does it matter now who understands and who does not? I have become a fallen flower!

Maasathuvan : Madhavi! My son gave this heap of gold for you. I will not touch it... to take it back would itself be wrong.

Madhavi : What need have I of wealth? After the treasure of my love has been lost... what use is this wealth that will perish? Come... tell me just once, “I have forgiven you!”

Maasathuvan : Madhavi... you are my beloved daughter-in-law!

Madhavi : Ayyo... Manimekalai! See what a good family your mother has destroyed, child! Look, child!!

[She weeps.]

Maasathuvan : Do not cry, child... come, give me the baby!

[He takes the child. The child smiles... tears gather in his eyes too.]

## Translation notes

- `மாதவி துறவு` is rendered as **`Madhavi's Renunciation`**, but the scene itself does **not** depict a formal religious initiation or ascetic vow. Here the title is carried by Madhavi's relinquishing of wealth, her rejection of material benefit from the relationship, and her emotional withdrawal after Kovalan's departure. The translation does not invent a ritual renunciation absent from the source.
- Kovalan's palm-leaf is translated as printed in this scene. Its wording overlaps the letter heard in scene 22, but the translation is made from the verified scene-23 text rather than silently harmonized to another version.
- `நாலைந்து பெண்கள்` becomes `four or five women`, preserving the source's approximate number.
- `மாற்றுக் குறையாத பசும் பொன்` is rendered **`pure gold that loses nothing under assay`**. The touchstone/assay metaphor is retained because Maasathuvan is not merely calling Madhavi `good`; he is declaring her moral worth through an image of unimpaired gold.
- `உதிர்ந்த மலர்` remains **`fallen flower`**, preserving Madhavi's self-image without translating it into a modern diagnostic or moral category.
- `எனக்கு ஏன் பொருள்?` and `அன்புச் செல்வமே பறிபோய் விட்ட பிறகு` retain the contrast between material wealth and the `treasure of love` she believes she has lost.
- Maasathuvan's answer does not merely say `I forgive you`; he calls her **`my beloved daughter-in-law`**. That relational acceptance is preserved exactly because it reverses Madhavi's self-description as the destroyer of the family.
- The source alternates speaker labels `மாசாத்துவான்` and `மாசாத்`; the English uses the stable character name `Maasathuvan` while the Tamil archival file preserves the source-label variation.
- No published English edition was used in drafting or reviewing this scene.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

The title can tempt a later reader to treat Madhavi's `renunciation` as punishment for her earlier life. The scene itself is more complicated. Madhavi actively returns wealth she says was accumulated without her knowledge and insists that she did not love Kovalan for property. Her act therefore rejects the economic arrangement that Chitrapathi openly exposed in scene 21.

Maasathuvan's response is equally important. He refuses the gold, calls Madhavi `pure gold`, and finally names her **his beloved daughter-in-law**. In a drama where characters have repeatedly used inherited sexual/social status to insult Madhavi, this scene grants her dignity through relationship and moral conduct rather than birth-status classification. That can be read alongside the anti-hierarchical and dignity-centred currents of the Dravidian/Self-Respect tradition.

At the same time, Madhavi remains deeply self-accusing—`I have become a fallen flower`, `your mother has destroyed a good family`. The translation does not erase that pain or convert the scene into a simple emancipation narrative. Its movement-era resonance lies in the challenge to money, status and inherited stigma, not in pretending Madhavi speaks later ideological language.
