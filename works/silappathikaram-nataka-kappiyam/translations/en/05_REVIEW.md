## 2. Kalaignar-language review

PASS.

The translation preserves the scene's sequence of registers:

1. descriptive Tamil landscape;
2. affectionate family reunion;
3. verbal play around `wealth`;
4. public celebration of Tamil-land abundance;
5. oral memory of Kannagi among hill people;
6. deliberate literary creation;
7. elevated transition into Puhar.

The English retains repetitions such as `There is, there is!` and `My husband will come, he will come`, and keeps the final geographical image `the Kaveri and the sea kiss each other` rather than replacing it with neutral topography.

## 3. Cultural / lexical review

PASS WITH ONE DELIBERATE RETENTION.

`குறும்பாடு` in the gift list is retained as `kurumpaadu` because the source term should not be silently forced into an uncertain modern zoological equivalent. The translation notes make that choice explicit. No gift item is omitted.

`ஆலோலப் பாடல்கள்` is retained as `aalolam songs`, because a generic `folk songs` would erase the source's named cultural form.

`கற்புக்கரசி` is translated as `queen of chastity`, preserving the source epithet while leaving broader interpretation outside the dialogue.

## 4. Tamil historical / dynastic framing review

PASS.

The translation retains:

- `மலைவாழ் தமிழர்களே` as `Tamil people of the hills`;
- `தமிழ்நாட்டு வளங்கள்` as `the riches of the Tamil land`;
- Chera, Chola and Pandya as separate named lands;
- `முடியுடை மூவேந்தர்` as `the three crowned kings`;
- the conscious literary act by which Sattanar tells and Ilango writes.

The scene therefore remains a Tamil historical-literary framing scene rather than being reduced to a neutral prologue to Kannagi's story.

## 5. Dravidian-context review

PASS WITH SOURCE/INTERPRETATION SEPARATION.

The interpretive note identifies defensible resonances in three source-supported features:

- explicit inclusion of hill communities within Tamil peoplehood;
- celebration of Tamil-land resources and geography;
- construction of a shared Chera–Chola–Pandya literary memory through the Kannagi epic.

The note relates these to Dravidian movement emphases on Tamil historical consciousness, civilizational continuity and literary recovery. No movement vocabulary is inserted into character dialogue.

Kannagi's centrality is noted as a source-supported fact, but the review does not force a later political programme onto her role in this scene.
