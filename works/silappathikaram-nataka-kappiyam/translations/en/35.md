# Scene 35

## The Pleading of the Case

### Pandya Royal Audience Hall (Outside)

[Kannagi comes, an anklet in her hand! The guards look upon her with authority and astonishment.]

Kannagi : Guards! Guards! Go at once and tell your king: “A woman has come to meet you—with tears streaming from kavi-flower eyes, a single anklet in her hand, and a form from which life seems to have departed.” Tell him at once! Tell him at once!!

[The guard goes inside]

Guard : O King! All tears and lamentation, a woman is trembling to see you urgently!

Pandya : Is that so!?

Bring her at once!

[Kannagi comes in like a volcano! The assembly is plunged into astonishment!]

Pandya : Who are you, Amma?... Who are you, appearing as the very form of anguish?

Kannagi : Poompuhar of Thirumavalavan Karikala Chola—proud to possess, as the very grammar of justice, the history of a king who gave his body for fame and won fame, and the great tale of Manu Needhi Chola, who placed his own son beneath the chariot wheel to guard justice—is my town!

Daughter-in-law of the great merchant Maasathuvan!

My name is Kannagi!

Pandya : What is your plea?

Kannagi : In the southern land ringed by three pearl-yielding seas, where the three murasus resound, the three Sangams are nurtured, beneath the shade of the three banners, Muthamizh is guarded by the lineage of the three crowned kings—O crowned king who has maimed the glory of that lineage! Before you hear my plea, know fully who I am!

Pandya : Who are you!

Kannagi : Kannagi!

Kannagi who has come with a single anklet in her hand! Kannagi who, with dust upon her body, loose black hair and knitted brows, dares to denounce Nedunchezhiyan Pandya, famed never to stray from justice! That is all you know of me!

O Pandya king! Your envoys are in the royal audience hall of Rome! Here, in the Pandya great assembly, Romans sit enthroned as warriors and envoys! You cross the seas, plant your flags and rule!

For that flagstaff to break and fall...

for foreign lands to speak in scorn...

know too, O King, know this: I am Kannagi, wife of Kovalan, slain after being subjected to the hasty judgment delivered by your tyrannous sceptre!

Pandya : Kovalan's wife!!! Wife of the thief who stole the anklet!

Kannagi : Enough! Whatever else you may fail to guard, guard your tongue, O king! Guard your tongue! Who is the thief? Is my husband a thief? No! The scoundrels who accused him of being a thief are the thieves!

Pandya : Do not close the eye of reason in anger, Kannagi! Your husband was caught with the stolen property in hand! You know, do you not, that the justice of Tamilakam has not spared a thief from death?

Kannagi : O Nedunchezhiyan Pandya who speaks the grammar of justice! In your land, what is called justice?

Is justice what the good man ordains? No! No! Here, what the powerful man ordains is called justice! That is why you have thrown the treasury of my life into a pit of death! O victor! O rider of the elephant beneath the parasol's shade!

Why did a case that should have been examined in the royal audience hall end at a temple mandapam?

Without giving value to the denials of the accused, why was the killing sword's work carried out in such haste?

Is this what you call justice?

Is this what you call rectitude?

Is this what you call fairness?

Is this what you call rule?

Can you be called a king who guards aram?

Pandya : Kannagi! What was in your husband's hand was the anklet of Queen Kopperundevi!

Kannagi : No! Not Queen Kopperundevi's anklet—it is my anklet, I who am Kovalan's queen!

Pandya : I cannot believe it!

Kannagi : Who are you to believe it? Who asks you to believe? I have come to plead a case against you! You are the guilty one! Therefore the right to pronounce judgment is not in your hand! Here are the great elders! They are

now the judges! Let them pronounce—a just judgment! Lords! Wise and learned elders! Do not bend the path of justice merely because Pandya possesses ornamented chariots, horses, men and a great army... Do not hide the wrong he has done behind the curtain of fame that says, “He overcame the Aryan army... all the world praised him...” Swear upon mother—upon Tamil—upon the people of the homeland—and pronounce judgment! Pronounce judgment!

The charge is that Kovalan stole Queen Kopperundevi's anklet!

For that, the punishment of death! A judgment given without inquiry... the king is responsible for it... that is my case!

Minister : Evidence for the case?

Kannagi : Here, this anklet! My bridegroom came to sell another anklet matching this one... Those great sinners killed him and left him there in a heap!

Pandya : If that is so, the anklets the Devi is wearing...?

Kannagi : One among them is mine... Do you want an identifying mark? You are desperate to ask me a thousand questions and make yourself innocent, O Pandya who destroyed the purity of the hall of justice!... If you had asked my dear, face to face, a question like this, the name `Nedunchezhiyan who never strays from justice` would have remained yours!

And my dear's life too would have remained!

Minister : Woman! Can you prove that one of the anklets the Devi is wearing is yours?

Kannagi : What use is proving it? Will you raise my dead husband and give him back to me?

Will you join his severed head back to him? You cannot... you cannot! But one thing... just as my dear's head drooped because life had gone from him, I shall make your heads droop too... because your honour has gone... because your rectitude has gone... because your justice has been destroyed.... Here, I make your heads too hang toward the ground...

Pandya of Madurai! In your wife's old anklet, are the pellets pearls or rubies?

Pandya : Pearls! Korkai pearls!

Kannagi : In my anklet are ruby pellets!

Pandya : Let me see... Devi! Give them here!

[The queen gives the anklets]

Pandya : [Throwing the first one angrily to the ground and breaking it] Here, look...

[From the first anklet—pearl pellets scatter]

Pandya : Here, the anklet Kovalan stole!

[He breaks that too.]

[Ruby pellets scatter from it...]

Pandya : Ah!

Assembly : Ah!

Kannagi : Still in doubt? Here is my other anklet!

[She breaks it in fury... ruby pellets spill out.]

Kannagi : What do you say now, Pandya? Ministers! Wise elders who speak aram! What answer is there? Have your heads drooped? Have Nedunchezhiyan Pandya's aram... martial valour... prowess... all of them mingled with the earth? Ayyo, why this distress? Tamilakam laughs, Pandya, seeing you stand with your head bowed!

The Tirukkural, the sacred-lore text of Tamil land, mocks you, Pandya, hearing that your justice has bent!

Where is your valour that leads the army without once lowering its head even if elephants and armies come against you by the thousands? Where is your majesty?

Where is your victorious sovereign gaze? All ruin! All of it ruin! Ruin! After all your honour is gone, what good are storied mansions... a gemmed mandapam? What good is a ruby-inlaid throne? For you who dispensed justice so that stain spread and the land scorned you, what use is a sceptre? What use a white parasol? What use the spear-force... sword-force... elephant-force? Useless man! One who came to teach disgrace to a warrior lineage!

Sinner who in a single moment destroyed the justice that had filled all Tamilakam, girdled by the sounding sea, without departing from it!

Weep! Weep! Weep well! O guardian who made me weep through all time, weep well!

Sinner who killed my love—one who dwelt within my eyes—my bridegroom who filled my eyes—making him writhe and writhe; weep! Weep! Weep!

Pandya : Kannagi!

Your husband is no thief! Without inquiry, in haste, instead of saying, “Kill and return—him!”... I gave the order, “Kill and return—he”...

I am the thief!
I am the thief!
O Tamil!
O homeland!
O my mother!
Forgive me!
Forgive me!

[He falls like a rootless tree and dies... Kopperundevi too embraces him]

Kopperundevi : O King!
O King!!
O King!!!

[So crying, she gives up her life upon him]

Kannagi : Let it perish! Let it fall and perish!! Pandya who strayed from justice! The only means that can wash away your stain is death alone! Death alone!! Let it perish! Let it be gone!! Madurapuri, which could not prevent my husband's death, be destroyed root and all! Let palaces become mounds of earth! Let the earth split! Let dust fly! Let fire spread! Let the blazing core of justice be seen!

[Terrible laughter... is heard throughout the city! Fire spreads through the city! People run through the streets.]

## Translation notes

- The opening verified source form `காவியுக நீருங்` is kept untouched in the Tamil archive. In its immediate classical image the English uses **`tears streaming from kavi-flower eyes`** rather than silently rewriting the source form before translation.
- `கொலு மண்டபம்` follows the established project convention **`royal audience hall`**. The English does not turn Kalaignar's royal scene into a generic modern courtroom.
- Kannagi's Poompuhar self-identification preserves the source's historical sequence. The first body-sacrifice king is **not supplied a name from outside the scene**; Manu Needhi Chola and Thirumavalavan Karikala Chola are named only where Kalaignar names them.
- The rhetorical accumulation `மும்முரசு / முச்சங்கம் / முக்கொடி / முத்தமிழ் / மூவேந்தர்` is retained as **`three murasus / three Sangams / three banners / Muthamizh / three crowned kings`**. The source `மூளியாக்க` is represented by **`maimed`**, preserving the charge that the king has damaged that inherited glory.
- `கொடுங்கோல்` is rendered **`tyrannous sceptre`**, deliberately contrasting with the righteous-rule force of `செங்கோல்` established in scenes 31–34.
- The Rome/Roman diplomatic and military imagery is preserved as printed: Pandya envoys in Rome, Romans in the Pandya assembly, sea-crossing and flag-planting. No external historical harmonization is added.
- `யாகாவாராயினும் நாகாக்க...` is translated independently from the Tamil quoted by Kalaignar as **`Whatever else you may fail to guard, guard your tongue`**. No standard published Tirukkural English wording is imported.
- `நல்லான் / வல்லான்` retains the sharp antithesis **`good man / powerful man`**. The following accumulation is kept distinct: `நீதி` → **`justice`**, `நேர்மை` → **`rectitude`**, `நியாயம்` → **`fairness`**, while `அறம்` remains **`aram`** where its wider ethical force matters.
- Kannagi's challenge to the king's authority is translated from the source's own royal-justice vocabulary. Terms such as `plead a case`, `guilty`, `judges`, `evidence`, `without inquiry` and `right to pronounce judgment` reflect words and actions present in the Tamil; no modern constitutional, due-process or rights vocabulary is imported.
- `பெருங்கேண்மைப் பெரியோர்கள்` is rendered conservatively as **`the great elders`** rather than assigning them a modern institutional identity such as a jury.
- The oath-sequence remains **mother → Tamil → people of the homeland**, and the command `தீர்ப்புக் கூறுவீர்! தீர்ப்புக் கூறுவீர்!` remains repeated.
- The anklet evidence is kept materially exact and in source order: the queen's old anklet contains **pearls / Korkai pearls**; Kannagi's anklets contain **ruby pellets**; the king breaks the first anklet, then the alleged stolen anklet, and Kannagi finally breaks her remaining anklet.
- `தமிழ் நாட்டு மறைநூல் திருக்குறள்` is rendered **`the Tirukkural, the sacred-lore text of Tamil land`**, preserving Kalaignar's Tamil ethical-literary framing without importing a standard Kural translation.
- `இமிழ் கடல் வேலித் தமிழகம்` follows the established image **`Tamilakam, girdled by the sounding sea`** rather than being flattened to a neutral geography.
- The king's source contrast between **`“கொன்று வருக அவனை!”`** and **`“கொன்று வருக அவன்”`** is deliberately not normalized. English represents the grammatical break as **`“Kill and return—him!”` / `“Kill and return—he”`**. The awkwardness is intentional because the source itself makes the `அவனை / அவன்` distinction part of his confession.
- `நியாயத்தின் பிழம்பு` is rendered contextually as **`the blazing core of justice`**, preserving the fiery image as Kannagi's curse turns immediately into the source stage direction of fire spreading through the city.
- The excavation photograph/caption on scan 84 and other image/pagination layers remain source provenance only; no dramatic English is invented from them.
- No published English edition was used in drafting or reviewing this scene.

## Dravidian movement resonance — interpretive note

This note is **interpretive context, not translated source text**.

Scene 35 is one of Kalaignar's fullest justice orations. Kannagi does not enter the Pandya court as a supplicant stripped of history. She identifies herself through Poompuhar and remembered Chola justice, then expands the frame through the three murasus, three Sangams, three banners, Muthamizh and the three crowned Tamil kings. Even the king's international standing—his envoys in Rome and Roman envoys in Madurai—is turned against him: outward prestige cannot protect a ruler whose sceptre has become tyrannous at home.

The central political argument is stated in Kannagi's own antithesis: here justice has become not what the `good man` ordains but what the `powerful man` ordains. She then removes the accused king from the place of judgment, appeals to the assembled elders, states the charge and the king's responsibility, and finally proves her case through the physical difference between pearl and ruby pellets. The scene therefore gives a woman not only grief or moral authority but argument, evidence, historical memory and the power to make a ruler bow his head. That strongly resonates with Dravidian-movement concerns with justice, anti-hierarchical reasoning, women's agency and resistance to humiliation.

Tamil itself is an ethical authority throughout the confrontation. Kannagi swears by mother, Tamil and the people of the homeland; later she invokes the Tirukkural as the sacred lore of Tamil land. When the evidence defeats him, Nedunchezhiyan confesses not to fate but to his own hasty command, names himself the thief, and asks forgiveness of Tamil, homeland and mother. The tragedy is thus made a failure of human rule and responsibility, not an explanation by destiny.

Kannagi's final demand for destruction remains, however, the furious climax of the character and the drama. Her calls for death, fire and the destruction of Madurapuri are preserved because Kalaignar wrote them; they are **not** presented here as a programme of the Dravidian movement. The interpretive relation lies in the justice argument, Tamil historical-literary authority, accountable power and the dignity of resistance—not in importing a later political doctrine into her curse.
