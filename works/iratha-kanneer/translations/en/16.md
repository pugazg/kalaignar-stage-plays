# Scene 16

[Arusal Man

The office of Arusal Man is locked. In the room beside its raised front platform, Muthan sits with his knees drawn up. It seems as though thousands upon thousands of thoughts are piercing and racing through his mind.

“Pazhuthur—thrown into the pit of slavery! And for that Pazhuthur, a Palaiyakkarar—the man who lost the town's fortress... That enslaved leader still has a little authority. And on that authority, the seal of arrogance. My Amma wore her body down to a shell working for that palace! And for that, what gratitude—what loyalty! Even if they have no wish to show gratitude, even if they have no mind to do good, could they not at least refrain from acts of destruction? They say there is no work for me with them—and that no place under their authority must give me work either.

In that palace I was a slave! No—I am no slave now! A man cast out beyond the reach of that arrogant tail of power. Does everyone born in the world live only in Pazhuthur? Is there nowhere else to fill one's stomach—to seek a way to live? But my Muthayi, more precious than the whole world, lives in Pazhuthur. Where can I go leaving her?”

Muthan is speaking to himself when a voice calls, “Muthan!” He looks up. Thirusangu stands before him.

Thirusangu: Don't be afraid, Muthan!

Muthan: Afraid of what? Has some new order been born in the palace?

Thirusangu: The palace has nothing to do with my coming!

Muthan! I know very well what happened that day in the grove. I put all the blame on you only because Sukadev must not think wrongly of Muthayi. The world excuses such things in men. But once a woman gets a name for losing restraint, it never disappears—remember that!

Muthan: What have you come to speak about?

Thirusangu: I know that there is true love between you and my daughter.

Muthan: At least you know now!

Thirusangu: Listen, Muthan! You alone must save my honour. Will you say yes to what I ask?

Muthan: What are you asking?

Thirusangu: Muthayi has said she cannot marry anyone except you.

Muthan: There is nothing surprising in that!

Thirusangu: I have come to you because...

Muthan: I understand, to some extent.

Thirusangu: I know that both of you have decided to marry.

Muthan: Even so, you want to ask for my consent once more.

Thirusangu: Muthan! Do you know how I dream that my daughter should live?

Muthan: Our life will go as you wish it to.

Thirusangu: It will not, Muthan—it will not! You cannot make it so. That is why I have come to beg you—for my pet parrot! Listen. Even now, if she chose, Muthayi could live in the Palaiyakkarar's palace—in a hall of marble. But she has fallen into the net you cast.

Muthan: I cast no net, and she did not fall into one—we are two pigeons caught together in the net of love!

Thirusangu: Cut that net and free my daughter. And after freeing her, do not become the hawk that chases her!

Muthan: She will not be far enough away for me to chase! Muthayi is the parrot perched on my shoulder.

What, are you thinking, “How shamelessly this prospective son-in-law talks to his father-in-law?” Forgive me.

Thirusangu: Muthan! Do not ruin a family. You have no means to live—you cannot sustain a life. Leave me aside... What father possessed by madness would agree to give his daughter to a poor man like you? Can you run a household? You too are a man—have you lost all sense of honour that you would marry and then lie waiting upon your father-in-law's house?

Muthayi is a young girl. She knows nothing of the world. She talks of love now! After marriage, all this love and romance will shut up shop. Diamond earrings for the ears, a pearl necklace for the neck—that is how a woman's foolish mind will change! Can you buy all that for my daughter? Leave a household aside—when the marriage itself is fixed, do you even have one gold coin to place before the priest as dakshina?

Muthan! Do not think I am speaking out of anger! I will tell you one more thing. You worked at the palace—that too is gone! You groomed the post-house horse and, as your wage, lived in this room no bigger than a palm—that work too you will not be allowed to do any longer! The palace order forbids it! From now on—the shade of houses—the shade of trees—those alone are your dwelling places.

Do not grieve that I am mocking your poverty! Along with all this sorrow, I will tell you one more thing so that you may understand. Suppose you were older, used to living according to the ways of the world, and suppose you had a son. Would you marry that son to a bridegroom whose father's name was unknown?

Whatever you might do—I cannot. Would my orthodox mind permit it? Think! You know only that your mother was Kaveri. As for whom she bore you to...

Muthan: Ayyo, enough!...

Thirusangu: There is no use closing up the story—no use opening your eyes wide. Open the door of thought! Open it just a little—that will do! Prosperity and splendour, a beautiful mansion, are calling my daughter! Will you deny her that? Will you, Muthan?

If you deny it, that is not a sign of your love for her. It is a sign only of your cruel desire to ruin her! I beg you—if need be, I will fall at your feet. Forget her. Otherwise Muthayi must forget her father. And if she does, this Thirusangu's corpse, hanging orphaned from a tree branch between earth and sky, will curse you—the corpse will curse you!

Muthan: Ayya! You ask me to forget my life itself—I will forget it.

Thirusangu: [overflowing with joy] Muthan!

Muthan: Go on your way.

Thirusangu leaves in great satisfaction. Muthan begins walking back and forth inside that tiny room. If he had walked that same distance on a road, he might have crossed many leagues!

## Translation notes

- Immediate authority: closed Tamil scene 16.md.
- The source-visible location form “அருசல் மண்” is transliterated as Arusal Man rather than silently normalized to a different Tamil heading.
- Thirusangu's poverty, lineage and orthodox-social arguments—and his suicide threat—are retained without endorsing or softening them.
- The translation preserves Muthan's “two pigeons in the net of love” reply and the repeated corpse-curse rhetoric.
