# Scene 27

[A street in Pazhuthur

Vetrivelan's military procession moves past the Palaiyakkarar's palace. A large crowd follows behind, watching the spectacle. Vetrivelan and Vedhalam sit in the carriage talking. Vedhalam is filled with pride—and a sense of protection—because he is riding in the commander's own carriage. He looks at the people with his head held high, a strange smile playing across his grotesque face.

Soldiers ride on either side, guarding the palanquin-carriages. Martial instruments meant for military formations sound with great pomp. In that richly adorned procession, only one man walks with his head bowed, unable to bear the humiliation he carries.

From the top of the palace, a figure watches the procession. It is none other than Poongavanam. Her eyes fall upon Muthan walking through the street in fetters. She cannot bear the sight. She strikes herself hard across the face. Tears pour from her eyes—she runs to her bed—falls upon it—and weeps.

How many times has she wept like this! As always, the three figures that had silently watched her—the love-birds, the cow longing for its calf, and the tiger in deerskin—are watching her even now.

The procession continues.

At the entrance to Thirusangu's house, Sumathi and Muthayi stand ready to watch.

The procession draws near. The war music passes beyond their house. The palanquin-carriages carrying the newly recruited men also pass. Next, the carriage carrying Vetrivelan and Vedhalam approaches. From the carriage Vedhalam points Muthayi out to the commander.

Vedhalam: Valorous lord! There she stands—the one cast in gold, the Muthayi I told you about!

Vetrivelan: Ah! Is that she? You called her “cast in gold”! In a poet's language one would call her a swan! One would call her pure gold! Ah! Cheeks shining like the places touched by a chisel upon refined gold! A curved parrot-beak waiting to peck her lips, mistaking them for kovai fruit! A mango-hued body created by Mayan only to enchant men!

Vetrivelan can no longer remain seated in the carriage.

Muthayi and Sumathi have been watching the splendour of the procession. The commander's carriage too moves past their house. And immediately after it—Muthayi sees him! Yes, Muthan who fills her heart.

The procession—the soldiers in it—the watching crowd—the roar of music—none of it remains in her awareness. She rushes into the middle of the street screaming:

“Aththan! Aththan!”

“Muthayi!”

The thunderous sounds suddenly stop. A terrifying silence.

Muthayi: Ayyo! Let him go! What cruelty is this! Aththan!

At that moment Vetrivelan gets down from the carriage and comes there.

Vetrivelan: Who are you, girl? What is this?

Muthayi: Lord! Let him go!—

Muthan: Muthayi! You are asking the cat to release the parrot!

Vetrivelan: (glancing sideways at Muthayi) Hey, Muthan! You are not the parrot!—

Muthayi: Aththan! After I begged you so much, you deceived me in the end—

Muthan: What can I do? It happened unexpectedly—let it be. I am going.

Muthayi: Go! If you have no wish to be freed, tell them at least to throw a pot onto my chest.

Vetrivelan: Tch! A knife upon fruit?

Muthan: Muthayi! At least understand that the fault is not mine!

Muthayi: Then whose is it? Did what I sent never reach you?

At that moment Thirusangu enters, furious.

Thirusangu: Shameless girl! Come here! You are making a public laughing-stock of my status.

Muthayi: Appa! Let me go. Let me go, Appa—let me go! Let one of these passing carriages run over me and kill me. They are taking away my very life—what use is this walking corpse now, Appa? Let me go!

Screaming, Muthayi falls in the street. Thirusangu grabs her and drags her away violently. Her golden body scrapes along the earth. Vetrivelan returns to his carriage. The instruments begin again. The procession moves on.

## Translation notes

- Immediate authority: closed Tamil scene 27.md.
- Vetrivelan's objectifying description of Muthayi is translated as source dialogue, not endorsed or softened.
- The unusual source phrase about throwing a pot onto Muthayi's chest is retained literally enough to avoid inventing a different object or action.
- The interruption of the procession, Muthan's cat/parrot warning and Thirusangu's public dragging of Muthayi are all preserved.
