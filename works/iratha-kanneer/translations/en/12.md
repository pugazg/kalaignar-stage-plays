# Scene 12

[Poongavanam's mansion

She cannot sit still in one place. She keeps moving restlessly here and there. Her heart pounds. She looks as though she has been seized by some terrible emotion. Without realizing it she mutters something under her breath. A lamp burns dimly. She turns it up until it glows brightly. In that light, the tears gathered in her eyes can be seen clearly.

Her mansion is arranged with precision—without excessive extravagance, yet lacking nothing in aristocratic ornament. Tall golden stands hold beautiful porcelain figures. How beautifully the sculptor has captured two pigeons sitting together as a pair! Next is a calf leaping and playing at a distance, while a cow tied by a rope suffers because it cannot reach the calf and lick it with affection. Ah! What a beautiful work of art. The elegance of those Chinese porcelain dolls is elegance itself!

A tiger sits draped in a deerskin. But the hide is not enough to cover its whole body. Such a figure decorates the room.

Poongavanam has seen all these life-filled figures many times. Yet even now she keeps looking at them. Her eyes repeatedly circle back toward the doorway. Her ears strain for the sound of footsteps. “Will Muthan come?” Her heart beats with that longing. She sits—gets up—stands—walks—tires—sits again—leans upon the bed.

A sound is heard near the outer door. She rises suddenly. Yes. He has come. Muthan has come.

Poongavanam tries to speak—but cannot. Muthan too turns to wood. Sweat beads and runs over his body.

With difficulty Poongavanam composes herself and begins to speak.

“Come—Muthan—come!”

“Why did you call me?”

“I'll tell you—come closer.”

“No—I will stay here.”

“My dear! Come near me!”

“Ah!”

“Don't be afraid.”

Muthan can no longer remain there. Poongavanam takes one step toward him. That is enough—Muthan begins to run outside. “Muthan! Muthan!” Poongavanam cries. He does not stop. Poongavanam runs back, falls upon her bed and begins to sob.

Poor thing: those Chinese porcelain figures remain standing around her just as before.

The love-birds, the cow longing for its calf, the tiger in deerskin—the three figures go on offering their permanent feast of art, unconcerned either that Muthan has fled or that the heart of Younger Queen Poongavanam is playing the raga Mukhari.
