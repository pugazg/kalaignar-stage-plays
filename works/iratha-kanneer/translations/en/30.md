# Scene 30

[Thirusangu's house

Poongavanam is weeping there. Here Muthayi has been thrown to the very edge of suffering and is writhing in pain.

Who is left to support her now? Her father is waiting for the time when, in his eyes, she will return to the proper path. Whatever obstacles came, the sight of her lover's face had been enough to reduce them to splinters. Now there is no way even to receive that comfort.

One hundred gold coins—the money her father had saved for the expenses of his daughter's procession and marriage festivities. She dared to give it up to rescue the man who was hers. Who was the deceiver who came between them? Their life in love had been like the water of a pond in clear stillness—and circumstance has thrown a stone into it! Eyes of sorrow seem to strike her from all eight directions. She can do nothing except cry. Perhaps opening her mouth and wailing aloud gives her some relief.

Sumathi comes in. She receives no welcome. With a mouth full of affection, she calls, “Muthayi!”

Muthayi: Why have you come here, murderess!... If you had asked me because you wanted it, I would somehow have found not a hundred but five hundred gold coins for you! For a miserable hundred gold coins you separated the two of us—sinner!

Sumathi: Muthayi! Is that what you think of me? Listen to what I say.

Muthayi: Moodevi! Do not show your face to me! Go away!

Sumathi: Muthayi!

Muthayi: If I say go, go! Wicked woman! What harm did I ever do to you—why did you take revenge on me like this? You acted as though you were full of affection. I never knew then that you would betray me like this!

Sumathi: Muthayi!

Muthayi: Do not speak, destroyer! Get out of my sight at once!

Sumathi: I am going. A time will come—then you will understand me!

Her eyes clouded with tears, Sumathi leaves.

## Translation notes

- Immediate authority: closed Tamil scene 30.md.
- Muthayi's accusation rests on her false belief that Sumathi betrayed her; the English preserves that dramatic misunderstanding.
- Moodevi is retained as the source insult rather than replaced with an invented modern equivalent.
- The hundred gold remains explicitly the marriage-festival savings stolen by Vedhalam in the preceding plot sequence.
