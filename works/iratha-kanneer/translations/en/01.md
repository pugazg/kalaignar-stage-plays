# Scene 1

[Pazhuthur

A river runs around the town. No one seems to have cared about its banks. Along the river lie unattended groves heavy with fruit. Enter the town from there and one finds broad streets that have lost their beauty; filthy water stagnates in their hollows. Piglets wallow in it, while children play by throwing stones. There are huge houses, a temple with a lofty tower, and beside the temple a large monastery. In the middle of the town stands a strong palace with upper storeys. Beside the palace stretch drab streets that seem wholly out of place. Horse-drawn, palanquin-shaped carriages come and go. Elderly men and women carry baskets of vegetables on their heads, crying out their wares. In the sacred tank opposite the temple, young men swim and play; maidens with lotus-like faces move about.

On the opposite bank of the tank stands an old, weathered house. On its front the dust-covered words “Anjal Meen” can be seen. At the entrance a well-built young man is rubbing down and bathing a magnificent horse. His shoulders look capable of challenging a mountain. His face carries the stamp of courage. With his lips pursed, he whistles something like a flute melody while throwing himself into the work of grooming the horse.

Inside the house is a man whose face, scored by the lines of time, makes it hard to say whether he is merely middle-aged or already old. His sharp eyes move this way and that as he rolls a sealing instrument in his hand. In front of him lie heaps of palm-leaf letters tied into bundles. He looks at them once. The movements of his thick brows, contracting and spreading, reveal an extraordinary hatred flaring within him. Sealing wax is melting in the fire before him. He looks once at the seal in his hand, sets down the instrument bearing a tiger emblem, and opens his broad mouth.

“Hey, you puppy of a wretch! Where have you gone? Come here quickly!”

“I’m coming!” a voice answers. A huge, rough-looking man comes from inside and stands respectfully. He carries a spear-staff with little bells tied to it.

He asks humbly,

“What must your servant do?”

“Do I have to tell you that too? Put the palm-leaf letters into the bag!”

The letters are put into the bag and tied.

“Seal the neck of the bag!”

The seal bearing the tiger emblem is applied.

“Hmm—pick it up and run!”

He lifts the bag and starts out.

“Hey! Puppy!... Be careful! One hundred palm-leaf letters—fifty gold coins! You must reach Mayanur before dark. Otherwise some thief on the road will snatch the lot and disappear.”

“All right! I’ll run as ordered.”

“Hmm! Go!... Yes... is the horse ready?... Hey... Muthan!... Is the work done?”

“It’s done! The horse is ready!” Muthan says as he comes in. Muthan is the young man who had been grooming the horse. Tired from the work, he sits on the raised platform by the street and lets out a deep breath. The rough man slings the bag of palm-leaf letters over his back, holds the bell-ringing spear-staff in his hand, mounts the horse and sits astride. The horse begins to fly down the road. Muthan keeps his eyes fixed in the direction it has gone. Four or five townspeople enter the post house.

Looking at Muthan, they ask,

“Muthan! Is the postmaster here?”

“There he is, sitting inside! Can’t you see?” Muthan says, pointing within.

An elder among the townspeople asks,

“Vedhalam! Is there any post for me?”

“No letter—but a small bundle containing five varagans has arrived.”

“Is that so! I’m very happy.” The elder receives the bundle.

“What luck! Doesn’t our Vedhalam get anything?”

“As though I would leave you out! Here!”

The elder unties the bundle, takes out a gold coin and gives it to Vedhalam. A broad smile spills across Vedhalam’s wide mouth. He hands the others the palm-leaf letters that have come for them and sends everyone away. Then, with a great yawn and an “Ahh!”, he picks up the little bundles lying before him and examines them closely. His lips mutter:

Maykanatha Swamigal!

Maykanatha Swamigal!

Maykanatha Swamigal!

Yes. He is simply reading the name written on each bundle. Muthan notices and looks at Vedhalam.

Muthan: Looks as though Maykanatha Swamigal has had quite a haul!

Vedhalam: They are all offerings sent by devotees!

Muthan: Offerings! Yes, yes! There is a name like that too for wages received without doing any work.

Vedhalam: There you go again, Muthan, beginning your supremely clever sermon!

Muthan: If I say something about the Swami, why do you get angry?

Vedhalam: Hey, Muthan! This is a post house! People are always coming and going. If you babble like this, you’ll get me into trouble. I knew something like this would happen—that is why I hesitated so much before renting you a room in my house.

Muthan: All right... all right... I’ll shut my mouth.

Vedhalam: Why are you still sitting there? Aren’t you going to the palace to groom the horses?

Muthan: Hmm—I’m going now. Are you afraid that if I sit here any longer I might say something else?

Vedhalam: No! If you take wages somewhere, shouldn’t you do your work properly?—That is all I meant. You’re a good boy—go ruin yourself however you like—what is it to me? I am leaving for the monastery. My duty matters greatly to me! Just don’t say anything about the Swami. He is not a choultry vagabond like you. He is a saint renowned throughout the world.

Vedhalam rises, taking the bundles of gold and the stack of palm-leaf letters with him.

## Translation notes

- Immediate authority: closed Tamil `../../scenes/01.md`; no OCR, PDF redrafting, later edition, web text or secondary English witness was used.
- The source-visible display wording `அஞ்சல் மீன்` is transliterated as “Anjal Meen” rather than silently normalized to another Tamil reading. Later source-secure `அஞ்சல் மனை` is translated by its dramatic sense as “post house.”
- `வராகன்` is retained as the coin denomination `varagan`.
- `சத்திரத்துப் பேர்வழி` is rendered conservatively as “choultry vagabond”; the insult is preserved without changing the Tamil source layer.
