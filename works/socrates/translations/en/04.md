# Scene 4

## Court

[The Athenian court of five hundred has assembled. Judges are seated on wooden benches. A few boards are covered with mats. Small rails set between the judges and the general public separate them. On the right side of the hall is the seat of the president of the court. Beside it is another seat. Both parties must come there and speak when it is their turn. On the left are two separate seats where the parties—and their witnesses—are seen. The seal on the box called `Hedge Log` is broken, and the charge sheets are poured out. The clerk reads the charge sheets.]

“Meletus, son of Melidayas of Pithanspettai, charges Socradas, son of Sobarannisko of Alopis, as follows. Socradas does not accept the gods accepted by this country and this government. He tries to corrupt the young through his teaching. He has been corrupting them. He must be given the death penalty.”

[Anytus and Lycon, like Meletus, also bring charges against Socradas.]

Anytus : Socradas!.... A soul that must not be allowed to move about in the country!..... That creature who finds fault with the democratic government has poison throughout his body!........ With words that bind those who hear—with speech that makes even those who have not heard desire to listen—with ideas poured out in profusion, this low-natured old man incites the young against the government and against the law!

[Socradas laughs aloud.]

President of the Court : Why are you laughing? What is the reason?

Soc. : Nothing, leader!....... In his anger Anytus—forgets himself and mocks me as an old man. I thought of that—and laughed! A head on which grey has fallen like sea-foam, for me—and for Anytus too!.... Isn't that so, members of the assembly!.... What, Anytus, isn't it true?

Mel. : Socrates!.... This case and inquiry are not about the heads of the two of you! ...I wish to remind you of that,

Soc. : Thank you very much, Meletus!.... But one thing!.... Just as the head is chief for this eight-span body....the head is chief for this case too! The knowledge that flashes out from my head—the meaningless imaginations that set out from your head to destroy it—the political arrogance that bursts forth from Anytus' head—the three-cornered struggle taking place among these three—that, Meletus, is what has produced this case!

Court : Socrates! What answer do you give to the charges laid against you?

Sar : Even a law that does not soften after hearing my answers may show mercy—but will this assembly feel pity for me?

Anytus : Did you see? He insults the dock and the assembly! This is exactly how he corrupted all the young men!

Soc. : How can an old man corrupt the young, man?.... Am I a woman for hire who casts a net for young men?.... A maiden who offers the feast of youth?

Mel. : A seductive speech not found even among women.... a captivating ornament of words not possessed even by women who cast nets for young men—word-magic—layer upon layer—these and many such tricks are arts you have mastered!........

Soc. : You may even call me a magician!........ Dear young man!.... Do I *sedukkirēn* the young? Or do they come and become corrupted on their own?

Mel. : You yourself go out deliberately and corrupt them!........ In marketplaces, at street corners, by the roadside, in the middle of streets, beneath trees where four people gather—there is hardly a time when your sermon does not take place!

Soc. : In the city of Athens I alone corrupt the young....is that so?

Mel. : Yes.

Soc. : You do not corrupt them?

Mel. : No!

Soc. : Anytus?

Mel. : No!

Soc. : The sculptor Lycon?

Mel. : No!

Soc. : These judges?

Mel. : No!

Soc. : No one corrupts the young; everyone does only good to the young, except me!....

Mel. : Yes—yes!

Soc. : When all these people—why, when the whole city of Athens—is reforming the young, can I alone turn them away from their path?........

Mel. : One drop of poison in a pot of milk!......

Soc. : Why, one could say it this way too.... One lamp in a dark house!.... What is it, Meletus, are you bewildered? If I attract the young bulls whom Anytus' display of political genius and your excessive imaginative poetry cannot attract—that distinction is not mine; it belongs to the principles capable of giving life to me and to the city of Athens!.... Members of the assembly!.... The reason the young circle around me like skylarks is not my ornamentation of words! Ideas whose abundance does not diminish—principles whose quality does not decline—thoughts like gold needed by this earth!........ They say I bewitch budding hearts with beautiful language and ornamental sequences. I ask them: that language does not belong to me alone—have I ever forbidden Anytus and Meletus from speaking it? Let them try speaking too—poor fellows, they spoke and lost! Not because their language lacked beauty—but because crookedness filled their thought! Because darkness settled over it!

I am trying, in a short time, to remove the darkness that has surrounded the country for many years! My reward for that is titles and accusations: ‘Socrates is an evildoer—an atheist who searches to find what is in the heavens and on the earth—a distorter who turns lies into truths’—all this! Never mind! I am over seventy, and for the first time I stand in the accused's dock; people such as Meletus and Anytus are desperately eager to have the death penalty imposed on me somehow. In some more years—some months—their wish may come true by itself! I have become that old! Before then, why they hurry to seek the unjust blame of ‘we killed Socrates’, I do not know.

Court : Socrates, stop speaking! Death—imprisonment—banishment—collection of a fine—many punishments such as these may be given to you. Which of them do you prefer?

Soc. : If I were to die, I could have been killed on the very day when the Council of Thirty ruled Athens—when I held office as one of its administrative members, and when the commanders were tried for failing to recover the bodies of the warriors who died on the Arjeena battlefield and the broken warships, I condemned the injustice of that council and walked out! After that, when the council sent me and three others to kill a man called Leenan, I refused and went home; for that refusal alone I could have received the death penalty then itself! Fortunately, if that assembly, the court of Greece, wishes to see Socrates' corpse, I cannot stand in its way!

You may ask me to undergo imprisonment—it is better to die than to lie in prison as the permanent slave of officials!

If I pay a fine, you will let me go! But if I had been rich enough to possess the means to pay such fines, I would have committed enough crimes to match that wealth! I can accept banishment—I can move freely in another country—but when my own.country refuses to accept my principles, what certainty is there that other countries will welcome them? Therefore, my dear great people of the city of Athens! As punishment for the things I have done, just as the winner of the horse-chariot race at Olympia is given a hero's grant, give one to me too and allow me to live happily! Continually provide the facilities needed for my campaign for ideals! That is the respect you should show to me and to my principles!

[The members of the assembly are stunned.]

Court : Insolent speech!.... Contempt!.... Let the vote take place! Death for Socrates?........Pardon?........ Cast your votes with conch-shells!

[On the board by the accuser and on the board facing the defendant, the members of the court place their “conch-shells” as they wish. The voting ends. The “conch-shells” are counted. 281 shells are counted for ‘death’ and 220 for ‘pardon’. The assembly looks at Socrates' face. A new light is born in his eyes.]

Court : 220 votes for pardon! 281 votes for the death penalty! According to the vote, Socrates must be given poison and the death sentence must be carried out!

Soc. : The court of the city of Athens gives a glorious judgment and smiles in silence! For the first time I have received the honour of becoming the object of this Athenian judgment, which will take its place in world history! For telling people, ‘Carry the lamp of knowledge in your hand,’ you have accomplished the holy deed of giving destruction as the reward! 281—220!........ If thirty-one votes had fallen the other way like this, Socrates might have been saved! I cannot refrain from saying that those votes too were bought by spending thousands of coins! No matter! How much greater it is to die speaking in my own way than to live by speaking in the manner of this court!........ I know: if a man is ready to say anything, do anything and ask forgiveness, he can certainly escape punishments like these! I never wished to become a warrior who falls at the enemy's feet on the battlefield. And so I have received death as my reward.

## Translation notes

- Immediate authority: verified Tamil `../../scenes/04.md` and, where needed, its verified page records; the 2009 published-English `Socrates` witness was not consulted.
- The source's `Hedge Log` is retained exactly as printed rather than corrected from external historical knowledge.
- The charge paragraph's source name variants are represented conservatively as `Melidayas`, `Pithanspettai`, `Sobarannisko`, `Alopis`, and `Socradas`; no external classical-Greek normalization is imported.
- The verified speaker label `சார் :` is represented as `Sar :` rather than silently regularized to `Soc.` or another speaker.
- The locked unusual source verb `செடுக்கிறேனா` is represented visibly as `sedukkirēn`; its first occurrence is not silently replaced by the expected neighbouring verb `கெடுக்க`.
- Locked source forms such as `இன்னும`, `கொன்றோம்என்ற`, `எனக்கேட்கலாம்`, `அபராதங்`, `என்னுடைய.சொந்த`, `எனதருமை ஏதென்ஸ் நகரத்தும்`, and `விட்டர்கள்` remain untouched in the Tamil archive; the English conveys their immediate sentence sense without claiming a Tamil emendation.
- The source explicitly says `முப்பத்தொரு வாக்குகள்` (`thirty-one votes`) despite the printed totals `281—220`; the English retains the source's thirty-one rather than correcting the arithmetic.
- `சங்கு` voting is retained as `conch-shells`, preserving the source's courtroom mechanism.
- The repeated martial, political and knowledge/light imagery is kept explicit, and the final source `*` is retained.
