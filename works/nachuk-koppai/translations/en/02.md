# Scene 2

Location: Maniyappa Mudaliar's house.

(Ayyar, Pazhaniyappan and Santha enter.)

O Father! Muruga! Shanmuga! Velayudha! Lord Kandavel!

Maniyappa: What is this? The sun is beating down so fiercely today. I had asked that Ayyar to come too. He still has not come. That man is always like this. Ask him to come for some auspicious business and he still will not turn up. All right, I will at least go as far as the street and come back.

(Ayyar enters.)

Ayyar: What, Mudaliar! Where are you setting out for?

Maniyappa: Come in! May you live a hundred years!

Ayyar: If Yama is fool enough to allow it...!

Maniyappa: Anyway, you have come—like the very god one sets out to worship appearing on the road!

Ayyar: Yes. At home they told me someone had come saying you had called me. I had just returned from Karamadai after performing a funeral rite and thought I could come in the morning. Before that, someone came saying our old Lakshmanan had died and that the death rites had to be done. I finished that too and came after a little rest. Even then, look—I sent my son to a naming ceremony and an ear-piercing ceremony before I came. After all, we need people like you, Mudaliar!

Maniyappa: Yes, Swami! I waited a long while for you and sent word through our Punniya Moorthi Ayyar. The fellow came back saying he had gone to a Brahmin feast. So I simply kept quiet.

Ayyar: Leave that aside! Why did you call me?

Maniyappa: We have to look at my daughter Santha's horoscope. That is why I called you.

Ayyar: Is that all? Nothing else?

Maniyappa: Hey! Who is that...? Bring that betel box!

Ayyar: No, Mudaliar. I do not have the habit. All right, bring the horoscope.

(Maniyappa goes inside and brings the horoscope.)

Maniyappa: What, Swami! Have you taken snuff?

Ayyar: Yes!

Maniyappa: Why does the snuff smell like chilli powder?

Ayyar: The snuff sold in the shop is not hot enough. So I grind chilli powder myself and mix it in. (Ayyar examines the horoscope.)

Ayyar: What, Mudaliar! Bring me glasses.

Maniyappa: Swami! All the dots on it are clear enough. What do you need a mirror for?

Ayyar: Hey! I did not ask for a looking-glass. I asked for spectacles.

Maniyappa: Spectacles? I do not have the habit of wearing them. Just look as it is.

(Ayyar examines the horoscope. Pazhani enters.)

Pazhani: What, astrologer-sir! The wind is blowing this way today. What is the occasion?

Ayyar: Nothing. Your father asked me to come about the child's marriage.

Pazhani: Father! Marriage...! For whom?

Maniyappa: Why do you ask like that? For our Santha, of course.

Pazhani: For Santha? To whom are you going to give Santha?

Maniyappa: To Azhagappan of Ariyalur.

Pazhani: What! To Azhagappan? Father! Azhagappan may consent. You may consent. The astrologer may consent, and so may the thirty-three crore gods, the forty-eight thousand rishis and the nine planets he speaks of. But is Santha's consent not needed?

Maniyappa: Hey, Pazhani! Santha is my daughter.

Pazhani: I am reminding you that she is my sister.

Maniyappa: She is my slave.

Pazhani: To love—not to authority!

Maniyappa: Do not recite the dictionary here.

Pazhani: That is how it will sound to you.

Maniyappa: You wretch, speaking against your father!

Pazhani: The situation demands it.

Ayyar: Pazhani, my boy! Listen to your father's words. Do not oppose what elders say.

Pazhani: Ayyar! This is a matter within our family. Do not interfere in this too.

Ayyar: All right, my boy.

Maniyappa: Hey, Pazhani! If you do not like this marriage, you and Santha can both leave this house.

Pazhani: Father! Is this your love? Santha wants to live a happy life. She seeks the bridegroom she desires. The very day her thoughts leapt toward the future, her eyes had already leapt toward Ekambaram across the street. These are not my words. I am telling you Santha's own words, Father!

Maniyappa: Pazhani, what “life” and what nonsense! You have begun to wax poetic. Am I throwing Santha into a well now?

Pazhani: You are not merely throwing her into a well, Father. You are going to lift a stone and drop it on her head as well. Listen to me. Let Santha garland the bridegroom she desires.

Maniyappa: Hey, Pazhani! Do you know whom you are speaking to? Love! Affection! Life! I knew about Ekambaram even then. I saw the looks and signals between them—that is why I hurried this marriage so much. Would I give my daughter to a mere betel-and-areca shopkeeper? And Ekambaram, writhing in poverty, as Santha's husband? You truly have gone mad.

Pazhani: Father! Does love stop to count poverty? Does it fear opposition? Is it not a slave only to affection?

Maniyappa: You are saying the same thing again. If you do not consent, perhaps I should leave the house and you can run the government yourself.

Pazhani: Can black stone be melted? Can crops be grown on hard barren ground? Can one show the sights of the earth to a blind man? No. You cannot run your rule much longer either. The time of its destruction is drawing near. Even a horse shut inside a stable is allowed a little respite. The women of this country do not have even that! Will no earthquake destroy this country? Will no smoking volcano boil over? Father! Do not forget that you are sacrificing Santha's life. The result will end disastrously later. This is my plea.

(Pazhani goes inside.)

Maniyappa: Swami! Did you see that?

Ayyar: I saw it. Am I the only one seeing it? You are seeing it along with me!

Maniyappa: You saw, did you? Then look at this. Swami, in what house would injustice like this happen?

Ayyar: Where would it happen except in your house? You must not simply let your children loose like this. The shastra says:

“A ghost will gather, white-erukku will bloom,
A netherworld creeper will spread—
In the house of one who hates Brahmins.”

Why, Valluvar himself sang it in the Kural!

Maniyappa: Swami! If things go on like this, where will my honour go?

Ayyar: It will have to board a ship and travel away.

Maniyappa: Quite right. If you come to know something, it is as good as the whole world knowing it.

Ayyar: Tch, tch! You think that of me...!

Maniyappa: What can I say, Swami? Teach them four crooked letters and they begin talking about love and brinjals! It was our mistake to educate them at all.

Ayyar: Come now, leave it. Young fellows these days are all like this. Some planetary defect makes them talk that way. After a little while everything will become all right.

Maniyappa: So the planets have hold of him now? Then they will not leave him alone. They will spin him around once for sure. Leave that. Look at the horoscope.

Ayyar: Yes. It is getting late for me. By the way, do you have the bridegroom's household horoscope?

Maniyappa: No, Swami! They did not send it. Just judge by the names.

Ayyar: What is the bridegroom's name?

Maniyappa: Azhagappan. The girl's name is Santha.

Ayyar: Azhagappan—Santha. Santha—Azhagappan! Ah! Ha! What perfectly matched names! When even the names match so beautifully, what other compatibility do we need, Mudaliar? Why, not even Parama Sivan and Parvathi, Mahavishnu and Lakshmi, or Saraswathi and Brahma were matched this well!

Maniyappa: Whatever it is, Swami, I have never harmed anyone. Bhagavan has always been on my side.

No need to be afraid.

Ayyar: You need not worry about anything. Your good time begins only now. As I left home, Garuda circled in the sky. That gave me complete satisfaction.

Maniyappa: They say the Garuda omen is a proven omen. All right, Swami, find a good day. The wedding must happen soon.

Ayyar: Mudaliar! If you finish it on Monday—Somavaram—between five-thirty and six-thirty, it will be good.

Maniyappa: Swami, even as you say it the gecko is calling. I do not like that day.

Ayyar: We can choose another day. I have to go somewhere else for a funeral rite.

Maniyappa: Swami! What is this? Sitting in my house, are you consulting auspicious timing for funeral rites and kalledduppu ceremonies?

Ayyar: What I mean is, apart from the days already promised elsewhere, we can agree only on the days left over. That is why I said it.

Maniyappa: Is that so? Then do as you please, Swami.

Ayyar: All right, it is getting late. I must go. Send your servant toward our house for a moment. I will write down all the things needed for the wedding and send the list.

Maniyappa: Then all right, Swami! I will give the money—

Ayyar: (Laughing.) What is the hurry for that now?

Maniyappa: Then all right.

Ayyar: Good heavens, before I could even say “put it in,” you have already put it in my purse! (Money is given.)

Ayyar: I will go and come back.

Maniyappa: All right, Swami.

(Santha returns from school.)

Maniyappa: Santha, my girl! Are you coming back from school?

Santha: Yes, Father.

Maniyappa: Have you finished studying everything, my girl?

Santha: There is still a little left, Father.

Maniyappa: You have studied enough. Do not go to school any more.

Santha: Why, Father?

Maniyappa: Arrangements have been made for your marriage.

Santha: Why do I need a marriage now, Father?

Maniyappa: Why do you speak like this, child? You are no longer a little girl. You are eighteen.

Santha: Father! Is this something that should be done only after asking me?

Maniyappa: Who else should be asked, my girl?

Santha: To whom are you giving me?

Maniyappa: To Azhagappan of Ariyalur!

Santha: Father! Your words are like forcing poison into a man's mouth and then asking him for his opinion about eating it!

Maniyappa: What is this cursed lament? You lie around sleeping at home. I think I should find a good family and get you married, and you begin talking philosophy.

Santha: Father! Why should I hide it from you? I have already given my heart to Ekambaram. To separate it from him now would be like separating Ayyar from a six-flavoured feast.

Maniyappa: You think you are going to win, so now you mock me as well. Even if you die, you will never marry Ekambaram!

Santha: Then arrange a marriage for my corpse.

Maniyappa: Do not try to frighten me with all this.

Santha: Father! A bridegroom suited to my heart...!

Maniyappa: I am telling you to change that very heart.

Santha: Why?

Maniyappa: Why? Not one reason, not two—I can give a thousand. Santha, in what way is Ekambaram superior to Azhagappan? He kept abusing Ayyar until the man had no way to earn even a single salli. All Ekambaram has today is one betel-and-areca shop. Marry Azhagappan and you can roll in money. You need never even step out of the car. There are any number of people waiting to carry out with their heads whatever task he assigns with his foot. Marry Ekambaram and you too will have to scrape along, counting every little expense. Listen to me, child. Why are you standing silent? Tell me what you want.

Santha: All right, Father. Do as you wish.

Maniyappa: That is how you should speak, my girl! See how happy I am now. Would I do something to ruin you? Be ready. I will take care of what must be done. (He goes inside.)

Santha: (Song.)

## Translation notes

- Immediate authority: closed Tamil `../../scenes/02.md`; no OCR, PDF redrafting, web text, later edition or secondary English witness was used.
- The priestly/astrological satire is kept direct, including the snuff/chilli joke, the invented “Valluvar” verse, name-compatibility reasoning, omens and fee-taking.
- `பிராமண போஜனம்` is rendered as “Brahmin feast”; `சல்லி` is retained as `salli`, a small coin denomination.
- The source-visible `களைத்திட` in Pazhani's stable-horse comparison is semantically unusual; English renders it conservatively as “allowed a little respite” and does not silently normalize the locked Tamil.
- `கருமாதி` is translated by context as funeral rite; `கல்லெடுப்பு` is retained as `kalledduppu` in the dialogue rather than over-specifying the ritual.
- The source colloquial sequence `ஒன்னுக்கு இரண்டு பத்திரவத்துக்கு மூணா` is difficult at exact phrase level; English preserves only the secure poverty contrast as “scrape along, counting every little expense” and does not alter the Tamil authority.
