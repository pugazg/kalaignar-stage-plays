# Scan 156

Printed page: 122

## Publication text

Socrates: These judges?

Meletus: No!

Socrates: So none spoils the youth; all do good to the young men, excepting me! . . .

Meletus: Yes, yes!

Socrates: While all these – why the entire Athens – try to correct the youth, can I turn them on evil path?

Meletus: A drop of poison is enough to spoil a whole pot of milk!

Socrates: Why, you can even say like this. A lamp for a house drowned in darkness! Why, Meletus, do you look flabbergasted? The political sagacity of Anitas, and your exuberant imagination-filled poetry . . . when these could not fascinate the young men, if you argue that I attract them, then the credit goes, not to me but to the principles of rationalism which keep Athens, the people, and me alive! Dear audience! The reason why the youngsters of Athens keep hovering about me like larks is not the beauty of my juggling with words! It is the never drying fount of constructive thoughts – magnanimous principles that never lose their standards – golden thoughts which the world most needs!

I am accused of enthralling the budding youth with my charming use of language, and jugglery with words. Let me question those who accuse me. That language is not my personal property. I never barred Anitas and Meletus from using the language! Let them also use the language and address the youth! Unfortunately, they have tried and miserably failed! Not because the language lost its natural charm . . . but because their thoughts were blackened with blemishes! Darkness has devoured them!

I endeavour to dispel in a short while the darkness that has surrounded us for a very long time! And this is the reward for it: ‘Socrates is an evil-doer – – What is there in the sky and on the earth, asks this atheist . . . a cheat who tries to change all falsehood into facts and truths-- - these are some of the appellations they give me! It’s all right! Anitas and Meletus are very eager that I, who am at the witness stand for the first time in all my seventy years of life, should somehow be sentenced to death! Their desire and expectation might get fulfilled on their own in a few years – or a few months! I have become so old! I wonder why they should try to earn the bad name that they murdered Socrates quite unjustifiably!

Chief Justice: Socrates! Stop your talk! Death penalty – imprisonment – exile – fine – any of these punishments may be imposed on you. Which do you prefer?

Socrates: If I were to die I ought to have been executed when I protested against and walked out of the senate of thirty members of which I was then a member . . . .
