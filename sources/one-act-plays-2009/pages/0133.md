# Preface

It is interesting to note that these one-act plays were mainly written for films. Writers like Kalaignar were inspired by Swami Sankara Das and Pammal Sambanda Mudaliar, the pioneering Tamil playwrights. Moreover, it was a common practice in the pre-Independence India to sing patriotic songs not caring for the propriety of the stage, nor caring for any relevance to the story. Such diversions were not treated as violations but welcomed by the audience.

One such device is introducing a play within a play, in the manner of some of Shakespearean plays. It was a welcome trend from the point of view of the spectators as it provided variety and enabled them to see the special talents of the artistes.

For writers like Kalaignar it served as a platform and a forum to discuss certain socially relevant topics, such as love between the prince and a low class woman as in *Anarkali*, or as in *Socrates* to spread rationalistic ideas, and the need to suggest and justify Periyar E.V.R’s social reform movement, or to maintain and project the past glory of Dravidian culture and valour as in *Cheran Senguttuvan*.

An important thing to note is that almost all sentences end with exclamation marks. Rarely does one come across a full stop. This attitude of the writer shows his intention to make all speeches declamatory, using highly emotive language. The style was also meant to suit the particular actor who was to deliver the speech. In most instances, it was the playwright’s close friend Sivaji Ganesan.

Talking about the style, mention must be made about the fondness of the writer to use a lot of alliteration, assonance and internal rhymes. Though it was practically prose, the tendency to make it poetic is pre-eminent, which invests Kalaignar’s language with its characteristic flavour and appeal and by which his propagandist material is transmuted into aesthetic stuff.

**M.D. Jayabalan**  
Reader in English (Retd.)  
A.A. Government Arts College  
Cheyyar
